# OnScript gold-set annotation guide

This guide tells you how to label the items in your packet. You do not need any background
on the project. Read section 1, then the task you are working on. Each task has plain
decision rules and worked examples drawn from real published statements.

## 1. What you are doing and what you see

OnScript reads public statements from members of the United States Congress and measures
which phrases many members use at the same time. A phrase that many members repeat in a
short window is a shared talking point. The system decides, by rule, whether each phrase is
a real political message or something else, such as a committee name or a piece of office
boilerplate. Your labels are the human answer key that measures whether those automatic
decisions are correct.

Each item in your packet shows exactly this and nothing more:

- The candidate phrase. This is a short run of words (three to six words).
- The full sentence that contains the phrase, with the phrase marked.
- One sentence before and one sentence after, for context.
- The release title, the member's office (state and chamber), and the date.
- For the claim-support task only, the set of other statements that carry the same phrase.

You never see the system's own guess, its rankings, whether it published the phrase, or any
later correction. That is deliberate. Your judgment has to be independent of the machine.

Some items are private-person material that the system suppresses. In those items the name
is replaced by a label such as `<private-individual-…>`. That label is a real part of the
item. Do not try to recover the name. Treat the label as the marker section 4 and appendix A
describe.

Work one item at a time. Judge the phrase in the context shown, not what you imagine the
member meant elsewhere. If two labels seem possible, the task's decision rules break the tie.

## 2. Independent pass and blinding

Every item is annotated by two people who do not see each other's answers. Do not discuss
items with the other annotator while the pass is open. Do not look up the live OnScript site,
because that would leak the machine's own decision back into your label. Record one answer per
required field for every item. Leave nothing blank in the required fields.

## 3. The six surface classes (task A)

Task A is the main label. Choose the one class that best describes the candidate phrase in the
sentence shown. Use this order. Stop at the first class that fits.

1. **Private.** The phrase names or describes a private individual who is not a public
   official, or the item shows a `<private-individual-…>` label. Choose private and stop.
   See appendix A for the name shapes.
2. **Nomenclature.** The phrase is an official name or title: a committee, a caucus, a bill or
   act, an office, an agency, or a place used as a proper name. A name is not a message even
   when the surrounding statement is political. See appendix B for the boundary with message.
3. **Procedural.** The phrase describes legislative, publication, or office process rather
   than a political position. Examples of the process register: introducing or cosponsoring a
   bill, a roll call vote, a markup, a hearing, joining a letter, or a line that only points
   to where a document can be read.
4. **Biographical.** The phrase describes a person's life history or career history: where
   someone was born, went to school, served, or was elected.
5. **Message.** The phrase carries substantive political meaning that a member is using to
   make a point. It states or argues a position, a value, a demand, or a characterization of
   an event. Most real talking points are messages.
6. **Unknown.** Use this when the phrase does not clearly fit any class above and is not a
   usable political message on its own. This covers fragments with no substantive content, a
   phrase that is only a reference to a document or body, and phrases you cannot resolve from
   the context shown. Unknown is the safe default. When you are genuinely unsure between
   message and something thinner, choose unknown rather than message.

A phrase is a **message** only when all three of these hold. If any fails, it is not a message.

- It contains a substantive content word, not only scaffolding, filler, or a reference.
- It is a complete enough unit to carry meaning on its own (see task B).
- Read the class rules in order. Message is only reached after private, nomenclature,
  procedural, and biographical have been ruled out.

### Worked examples for task A

**Message.** Phrase: `after the supreme court`. Sentence: "Congressman Jason Crow (D-CO)
released the following statement after the Supreme Court ruled to uphold the longstanding
constitutionality of birthright citizenship." Office: D, House, CO. Date: 2026-06-30. This
is a member framing a substantive response to a Supreme Court ruling. Label: message.

**Unknown (reference only).** Phrase: `the house of representatives`. Sentence: "Ciscomani
led this bipartisan, bicameral legislative package in the House of Representatives." Office:
R, House, AZ. Date: 2025-01-02. The phrase only names the chamber as a location for an
action. It states no position. Label: unknown.

**Unknown (points to a document).** Phrase: `letter is available`. Sentence: "Text of the
full letter is available HERE." Office: R, Senate, NC. Date: 2025-01-06. This is a pointer to
where a document can be read, not a message. Label: unknown.

**Nomenclature.** Phrase: `on energy and commerce`. Sentence: "He will serve on the House
Committee on Energy and Commerce and the House Committee on Judiciary." Office: R, House, SC.
Date: 2025-01-03. The phrase is part of a committee name. Label: nomenclature.

**Procedural.** Phrase: `roll call vote`. Sentence: "Nunn's resolution proposes a
Constitutional amendment prohibiting total outlays for a fiscal year from exceeding total
receipts for that fiscal year unless Congress authorizes the excess by a three-fifths roll
call vote." Office: R, House, IA. Date: 2025-01-03. This names a voting procedure. Label:
procedural.

**Biographical.** Phrase: `was elected to serve`. Sentence: "The Honorable Tom Barrett was
elected to serve as Representative of Michigan's 7th Congressional District." Office: R,

## 4. Phrase completeness (task B)

Record whether the candidate phrase is a complete enough unit to carry meaning on its own.

- Answer **yes** when the phrase is a coherent noun phrase, verb phrase, or clause that a
  reader could understand as a unit. `working families` is complete. `after the supreme
  court` is complete enough to place an event.
- Answer **no** when the phrase is a fragment that dangles: it ends on a preposition or
  conjunction that needs an object, or it starts mid-construction and only makes sense glued
  to neighboring words. `lower costs for` is incomplete, because it needs an object. `introduced
  legislation to` is incomplete, because the "to" needs a verb.

Completeness is about the phrase as shown, not the sentence. A phrase can sit inside a fine
sentence and still be an incomplete fragment on its own.

### Worked examples for task B

- `working families`. Sentence: "Republicans claim they are for working families, but it is
  Democrats who support an increase in the minimum wage." Complete noun phrase. Answer: yes.
- `lower costs for`. Sentence: "She will continue efforts to put people over politics, lower
  costs for families, create better paying jobs." The phrase stops at "for" and needs an
  object. Answer: no.
- `introduced legislation to`. Sentence: "Cramer co-chairs the Congressional Coalition on
  Adoption and introduced legislation to allow expectant mothers to receive child support
  payments." The phrase ends on "to" and needs a verb. Answer: no.

## 5. Cross-receipt proposition consistency (task C)

For an item that shows a support set, judge whether the other statements are really saying the
same thing with this phrase, or whether the phrase happens to appear in statements about
different subjects.

- Answer **yes** when the supporting statements use the phrase to assert the same underlying
  proposition or address the same event. On 2026-06-30, dozens of Democratic members used
  `after the supreme court` in statements about the birthright citizenship ruling. That is one
  shared proposition.
- Answer **no** when the phrase is a generic string that shows up across unrelated subjects.
  A phrase like a common committee reference can appear in statements about many different
  bills. The shared string does not mean a shared message.

You are judging the proposition, not the exact wording. Members will phrase the same point
differently. Same point, different words, is still consistent.

### Worked examples for task C

- Consistent. Phrase: `after the supreme court`, date 2026-06-30. The supporting statements
  are member reactions to the same Supreme Court birthright citizenship decision. Answer: yes.
- Consistent by proposition. Phrase: `the 14th amendment`, date 2026-06-30. Members write "as
  outlined by the 14th Amendment" and "guaranteed by the 14th Amendment to the Constitution."
  Different wording, same proposition about the constitutional basis for birthright
  citizenship. Answer: yes.
- Inconsistent. A generic procedural phrase whose supporting statements are each about a
  different bill or program shares only the string. Answer: no.

## 6. Stance consistency (task D)

Record the stance of the phrase in the sentence shown.

- **Affirmative.** The member asserts the phrase as their own position or as a fact they
  endorse. "Birthright citizenship is guaranteed by the 14th Amendment" is affirmative.
- **Negated.** The member states the phrase in order to reject or deny it, usually with "not,"
  "never," "reject," or "oppose." "This is not a stock trading ban" is negated. The words are
  present, but the member is disowning them.
- **Mixed.** Use mixed only when the same sentence both asserts and denies the phrase, or
  quotes an opponent's use and then rejects it in the same breath.

Watch for the contrastive frame. "Republicans claim they are for working families, but it is
Democrats who..." uses `working families` to set up a contrast. Read who is asserting the
phrase. If the member is reporting an opponent's claim in order to dispute it, that is negated
or mixed, not affirmative.

### Worked examples for task D

- Affirmative. `the 14th amendment` in "Birthright citizenship is guaranteed by the 14th
  Amendment to the Constitution." The member endorses it. Answer: affirmative.
- Negated. A phrase stated only to deny it, as in "this is not a stock trading ban." Answer:
  negated.
- Mixed. `working families` in "Republicans claim they are for working families, but it is
  Democrats who support an increase in the minimum wage." The member reports the opponent's
  use of the phrase and disputes it. Answer: mixed.

## 7. Document family (task E)

Give every item a family ID. Two items share a family ID when their releases are the same
document reused with local edits, or one document that many offices published together, such
as a joint letter. Two items get different family IDs when they are separate documents that
happen to share a phrase.

Rules:

- A joint or cosigned release that many offices put out is one family. Give every copy the
  same ID.
- A release that reuses another release's text with small edits (a name, a district, a date)
  is the same family.
- Two members writing their own statements about the same event are not one family, even when
  they share a phrase. Distinct authorship with distinct text is distinct families.
- Do not chain. If document A resembles B and B resembles C, but A and C are not themselves
  alike, do not force A and C into one family.

Use a short stable label of your choosing for each family, for example `fam-birthright-01`.
Reuse the exact same label for every item you place in that family. Consistency of the label
across items is what matters, not the label text.

### Worked examples for task E

- Distinct families. The many Democratic statements on 2026-06-30 about birthright citizenship
  are each a member's own statement with its own wording. Amo's statement and Crow's statement
  share the topic but are different documents. Give them different family IDs.
- One family. A single "Dear Colleague" letter cosigned by twenty members and published by
  each of their offices is one document. Give all twenty items the same family ID.
- Same family with edits. Two freshman members publish a near-identical "sworn in to the 119th
  Congress" release that differs only in the member name and district. If the text is otherwise
  the same template, that is one family. If each office wrote its own bio, they are separate.

## 8. Public claim support (task F)

For an item that shows a support set, judge whether the phrase has real public support: at
least three distinct offices genuinely asserting the same point with this phrase.

- Answer **yes** when three or more distinct offices assert the same proposition with the
  phrase (task C), counting one joint document as one office, not many.
- Answer **no** when the support collapses below three once you set aside copies of one joint
  document, off-topic uses, or negated uses.

The key trap is counting. If ten offices all published the same joint letter, that is one
supporting document, not ten. If two members truly wrote their own statements and a third use
is a negation, the real support is two, so the answer is no.

### Worked examples for task F

- Supported. `after the supreme court` on 2026-06-30 is carried by dozens of Democratic offices
  writing their own statements about the same ruling. Well over three distinct offices, one
  proposition. Answer: yes.
- Not supported (joint collapse). A phrase that appears to have many carriers, but they are all
  copies of one cosigned letter, has one supporting document. Answer: no.
- Not supported (off topic). A phrase carried by only two on-topic statements plus one use in a
  statement about a different subject has two real supporters. Answer: no.

## Appendix A: privacy name shapes

Some phrases name a private individual. The privacy floor removes these people from public
output, and the correct label for them is private. Learn to recognize the shapes so you label
them even when the item is not already masked.

- A full personal name of someone who is not a public official: first and last name together,
  sometimes with a middle name, used as the subject of a story rather than as a member or a
  named official.
- A name attached to a personal circumstance: a constituent, a victim, a patient, a defendant,
  a family member. The clue is that the person is being described, not speaking as an official.
- A masked span such as `<private-individual-abc123>`. This is already suppressed. Label it
  private. Do not try to recover the name, and do not treat the mask as an ordinary word.
- A residual fragment next to a mask, such as a lone surname left beside a masked span, still
  points at a private person. Label the item private.

Public officials are not private. A sitting member, a cabinet secretary, a governor, a judge
named in their official role, or a President is a public figure. A phrase built around a public
official's name and role is nomenclature or message, not private. When a name could be either,
ask whether the person is acting in a public office. If yes, it is not private.

## Appendix B: nomenclature versus message

The hardest boundary is between an official name (nomenclature) and a real message. Use these
tests.

- If removing the phrase would only drop a proper name from the sentence, it is nomenclature.
  "He will serve on the House Committee on Energy and Commerce" loses only a committee name.
- If the phrase carries a claim, value, or characterization beyond the name, it is a message.
  "We must defend the Fourteenth Amendment" uses the amendment's name to make an argument.
- A committee, caucus, bill, act, agency, or office title, used to say who sits where or what a
  body is called, is nomenclature even inside a political statement.
- Watch for names the automatic rules can miss. `committee on ways and means` in "Congressman
  Gomez sat on the powerful House Committee on Ways and Means" is a committee name, so the
  correct label is nomenclature, even though it reads like ordinary prose.
- Watch the reverse too. `born in the united states` inside "If you are born in the United
  States of America, you are a citizen" is not a biography of one person. It states the
  birthright citizenship principle, so the correct label is message. Judge the phrase by what
  it does in the sentence, not by a keyword it contains.
